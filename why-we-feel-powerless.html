ESSAY 03

Why We Feel Powerless — And What You Can Do About It

Feeling powerless is a documented psychological effect — staying powerless is a choice.

TL;DR: You're not powerless — you're part of the equation. History shows that small actions have sparked revolutions and social movements, and research proves that even small groups can shift the course of change. Here's how to reclaim your sense of impact and take meaningful steps forward.

Have you ever scrolled through the news, feeling overwhelmed by the endless stream of global issues — climate change, inequality, conflict — and wondered if there's anything you can truly do to make a difference?

You're not alone. Many of us feel powerless in the face of problems that seem too big, too complicated, and entirely out of personal reach.

That feeling isn't a personal failing — it's a well-documented psychological phenomenon called learned helplessness. First identified by psychologist Martin Seligman, it describes how repeated exposure to situations where effort seems ineffective can cause people to stop trying altogether — even when change is possible. And this mindset doesn't develop in a vacuum. Modern media amplifies powerlessness by presenting global issues as enormous, relentless, and detached from anything an individual can control. Over time, that constant exposure leads to disengagement — not because people don't care, but because they've been convinced their actions won't matter.

Underneath it all sits a subtle but powerful misconception: the belief that significant change can only happen from the top down. We've been conditioned to think meaningful impact requires vast resources, political influence, or corporate power. A 2020 study in the Journal of Personality and Social Psychology found that people are far more likely to disengage when global problems feel too large, abstract, or disconnected from everyday life. Researchers called it the "drop-in-the-bucket" effect — individual effort feels so small it seems pointless.

Here's what matters: the systems that perpetuate inequality, environmental harm, and injustice thrive precisely because individuals feel disconnected from their own power. As long as we see ourselves as isolated and ineffective, the structures that keep us feeling that way go unchallenged.

Your Actions Matter — More Than You Might Realize

Despite how it feels, individual actions can drive monumental change.

With nothing more than a pen and bold conviction, Thomas Paine sparked a revolution. His pamphlet Common Sense transformed complex political theory into sharp, accessible language that spoke directly to ordinary people. In a few pages, he stirred thousands to believe in the possibility — and the necessity — of independence.

Years later, Rosa Parks made her stand by sitting down. Her quiet act of defiance on a segregated bus became a seismic moment in the Civil Rights Movement, proving that courage doesn't always shout — sometimes it sits still. One decision became a rallying cry for justice across a nation.

And in a small classroom, a young boy named Ryan Hreljac learned that clean water wasn't a guarantee for every child. Moved to act, he raised money to build a single well. That simple gesture grew into a global movement — a reminder that compassion paired with action, at any age, changes lives.

Each of them lit the match on something far greater than themselves — not by forcing change alone, but by awakening something in others. Paine's words didn't win the Revolution, but they gave it voice and vision. Parks didn't dismantle segregation by herself, but her defiance inspired a movement to rise. Ryan couldn't solve the global water crisis as one child, but his compassion mobilized countless others to carry the mission forward.

That's the true power of individual action: not to carry the weight of the world, but to ignite the will of the world to carry it together.

Real change has never rested on one person's shoulders — it takes many hands, voices, and hearts. But it often begins with one bold spark. When someone taps into a shared sense of justice, dignity, or hope, they create momentum others can step into.

And this pattern isn't just anecdotal — it's backed by research. Political scientist Erica Chenoweth analyzed over 300 major resistance movements from the last century and found that nonviolent campaigns are twice as likely to succeed as violent ones. Even more striking: when just 3.5% of a population actively participates in nonviolent resistance, success becomes statistically near-inevitable. That's not a majority. That's a small, determined minority showing up and shifting history.

You can see it in real time, too. The viral #TrashTag challenge began with a handful of eco-conscious individuals picking up litter and sharing it online — and spread into a global cleanup effort. Community-Supported Agriculture started with small groups of farmers and neighbors; now CSAs sustain local economies across the country.

These aren't isolated success stories. They're proof. When personal action aligns with collective energy, impact multiplies. Change doesn't wait for perfection or permission — it waits for participation.

What You Can Do Today

Feeling powerless doesn't mean you actually are. The path from stuck to empowered starts with small, practical steps. Today, you can:

  • Question the narrative. Pay attention to how information is presented to you. Ask whose interests are served by making you feel helpless. Is the message designed to empower you — or keep you compliant?
  • Act locally. Identify one manageable issue in your community — food insecurity, education access, neighborhood safety — and commit to supporting or participating in a solution.
  • Join others. Find groups working on issues you care about. Collective action amplifies individual voices and multiplies impact.

The Shift Starts With You

Change doesn't begin when someone "in charge" gives the green light. It begins with your mindset, your daily choices, and your small courageous acts.

No single action will fix everything. But your small, intentional efforts don't exist in isolation — they ripple outward.

Feeling powerless is understandable. But staying powerless is optional.
